# What MuseScore Is

**The outcome:** you can say what kind of thing
MuseScore makes, how that differs from a recording,
and who reaches for notation software and why.

## Two kinds of computer music

A recording stores *sound*: pressure waves, sampled
tens of thousands of times a second. Play it back
and you hear the performance that happened. Change
anything — the key, the tempo, one wrong note — and
you are doing surgery on a waveform.

A score stores *instructions*: this instrument,
this pitch, this long, this loud. Nothing has been
performed yet. Change the key and every note moves,
cleanly, because nothing was ever frozen. Print it
and a human performs it; play it back and the
computer performs it, approximately, so you can
check your writing.

MuseScore is a program for the second kind. Its
proper name for the job is **notation software**,
and its current product name is **MuseScore
Studio** (the "Studio" arrived with the 4.x
generation's later releases; older writing just
says MuseScore). It is free and open source —
the program itself costs nothing, a fact that
matters to the school bands, church choirs,
community orchestras and bedroom arrangers who
are most of its users. It is developed alongside
**musescore.com**, a separate commercial service
for sharing scores, which chapter 8 treats
honestly: the program is free; parts of the
sharing site are a shop.

## Who writes scores

- **Teachers and students**, making exercises,
  parts at the right difficulty, and arrangements
  a school ensemble can actually play.
- **Band and choir directors**, transposing a
  song into the key their singer can sing, or
  writing out the one missing part.
- **Composers and arrangers**, from first sketch
  to a printed set of parts.
- **Transcribers**, turning a recording into
  notation by ear — the program holds the
  notation; the ears are still yours.
- **Publishers of one** — anyone who wants a
  lead sheet, a hymn sheet, a wedding song,
  printed properly instead of pencilled.

## What a score knows that audio doesn't

A score separates things a recording mixes
together. The *pitch* is distinct from the
*spelling* (F sharp and G flat sound alike on a
piano and are different instructions to a
violinist). One staff can hold several **voices** —
independent lines sharing a staff. A note knows
its instrument, its part, its place in a repeat
structure. That structure is why a score can be
transposed in a second (chapter 9 does it and
verifies every pitch), why each player's **part**
can be lifted out of a full score (chapter 6),
and why a score file is small: this guide's
eight-bar melody, with full layout, is a
31-kilobyte archive, while 32 seconds of its
rendered audio is 11 megabytes.

## The honest paragraph about other people's music

Notation makes copying easy, and the internet
makes other people's scores easy to find.
Easy is not the same as permitted. A song's
composition is somebody's copyright long after
the recording is old; arrangements of it are
works in their own right; a score you may
download for private study may not be yours to
perform in public, hand to a paid band, or
republish. MuseScore the program takes no
position and checks nothing. The position is
yours to take: write your own, use public-domain
sources (old hymnals and classical editions are
the rich vein), and treat found scores of living
composers' work as reading material unless a
licence says more. This guide's fixture melody
was invented by its lab, so every measurement
in it can be shared freely.

## Where it sits

Notation programs divide roughly by how the
music gets in and what it costs. MuseScore is
the free, graphical one: you place notes with
mouse and keyboard and see the page as you
work. Chapter 11 sets it beside its neighbours
— audio tools that solve a different problem,
a text-driven engraver for programmers, and
the commercial notation programs whose prices
explain MuseScore's existence. First, the
moving parts.
