# Exporting, Printing and Sharing

**The outcome:** your score leaves the program
in the right form for its destination — a
printed page, an image, a sound file, or the
web — and you know which knobs the lab
measured on each door.

Everything here is **File → Export** in the
window, or the converter of chapter 9. The
formats divide by destination.

## For print: PDF

PDF is the printed page, frozen: same
pagination, same fonts, everywhere. It is
what you email the band, upload to the print
shop, and archive against the day the
notation program is gone.

Measured on the fixture: the PDF is **one
page, A4 (595 × 842 points)** — the
program's default page size; set yours in
the score's style if your paper differs.
With parts created (chapter 6), export can
produce the score and each part; choose
deliberately, because "all of it" for a big
score is a document of hundreds of pages
and exactly what a librarian wants, while
the second clarinet wants only her twelve.

Print the PDF from a PDF reader, not from
the notation window: what the reader shows
is byte-for-byte what the printer gets.

## For screens: PNG and SVG

- **PNG** is a picture of a page. The lab's
  export measured **9924 × 14028 pixels** —
  1200 DPI at A4, the default image
  resolution, which is print-shop sharp and
  wildly more than a web page needs. Set the
  resolution down (chapter 9's `-r`, or the
  export dialog) for anything headed for a
  screen, unless you enjoy 900 KB thumbnails.
- **SVG** is the vector form: infinitely
  scalable, the right choice for a score
  excerpt inside a document or web page.
- Both are written **one file per page, with
  the page number in the name**: ask for
  `first-light.png` and you receive
  `first-light-1.png`. Chapter 13 exists
  largely because of this sentence.

An image of a score is a photograph, not the
score: nothing in it can be edited, searched
or played. Send images for looking, never
as the only copy.

## For ears: audio and MIDI

Audio export (chapter 7) renders the
playback: WAV for quality, MP3 for size,
FLAC and OGG also on offer in the dialog.
MIDI export sends the performance sketch to
other music software (chapter 5). Both are
exports of a *performance decision* — the
current sounds, the current tempo, repeats
played — not of the document. A changed
mind about tempo means re-exporting; the
`.mscz` doesn't care.

## For people: musescore.com

The sharing site (separate from the program,
run commercially by the same project) is
where scores are published to the world:
upload from the program (**File → Publish**)
or the site, with an account, and your score
gets a page with an in-browser player, a
following button, and an audience of
strangers who were searching for exactly
that song at 2 a.m.

The commercial side, stated plainly because
chapter 1 promised: the program is free and
open source; the site is a business. Your
own scores upload free. The site also sells
a subscription and licensed "official"
scores from publishers; downloading *other
people's* scores in print-ready form has,
at various times, sat behind that paywall,
and the exact terms move — read them on the
site, at the time, rather than trusting any
guide (this one included) about a shop's
current prices. And chapter 1's point
applies with full force here: publishing an
arrangement of someone else's copyrighted
song to the world is a rights question, not
a button.

Offline sharing needs no account and no
site: a PDF and an MP3 in an email have
carried more music to more musicians than
any platform. The site is for discovery;
the files are for keeps.
