# Your First Score

**The outcome:** you can start a score, put
notes into it, hear it played back, and save
it — and you know the three habits that keep
scores editable instead of fragile.

> A note on evidence. This chapter teaches the
> *graphical* craft — clicking, typing, dragging
> notes onto a staff. This guide's lab ran the
> graphical program exactly once, to photograph
> it with a score open (chapter 2), and entered
> no notes by hand; a headless lab has no hands.
> The steps below follow the program's own
> handbook and describe the current (4.7)
> interface. Treat them as documented, not
> lab-measured — the lab-measured chapters are
> the ones about files, printing and conversion,
> where every claim carries its numbers.

## Starting

**File → New** opens a wizard: choose
instruments (or a template — piano, choir,
band, orchestra), then key signature, time
signature, tempo and title. Every choice here
is changeable later; the wizard just saves
you setting them one by one. A single melodic
instrument (flute, voice) is the kindest
first score: one staff, one line, nothing to
coordinate.

Give the score its title and composer in the
wizard or later under **File → Score
properties**. These are not decoration: they
are typeset onto the page, stored in the
file, printed by chapter 9's metadata tools,
and they are how future-you tells two files
apart.

## Entering notes

Two honest ways exist, and most writers mix
them:

- **Mouse:** pick a note value in the toolbar
  (or press its number key), click on the
  staff. Slow, certain, and how everyone
  starts.
- **Keyboard note entry:** press `N` to enter
  note-input mode; letter keys name pitches
  (C, D, E…), arrows adjust the octave and
  spelling, digits set the duration first.
  After twenty minutes of practice this is
  several times faster than the mouse, and
  after an hour it is not close.

Rests, accidentals and dots are toolbar or
keyboard affairs in the same mode. Chords are
built by adding notes to a note (Shift with a
letter, in keyboard entry). To fix a mistake,
select the note and change it — do not delete
and re-enter unless the rhythm itself is
wrong; editing in place keeps everything
attached to the note (lyrics, articulations)
attached.

**Voices:** when two rhythms must share a
staff (a melody over a held chord, two singers
on one line), put the second line in **voice
2** with the voice buttons in the toolbar.
Voices are the feature beginners discover
last and need earlier than they think;
a measure that "won't add up" is usually one
voice being asked to do two rhythms' worth.

## The habits that keep scores healthy

1. **Save in `.mscz`, early and often**
   (Ctrl+S). The native format keeps
   everything. Export copies for other
   purposes; never let an export become the
   only copy (chapter 5 explains what each
   format keeps).
2. **Enter cleanly before you decorate.**
   Pitches and rhythms first, played back
   once; then dynamics, slurs, text. A score
   that sounds right undecorated is a score
   whose problems are findable.
3. **Use repeats as repeats.** A section that
   repeats should use repeat barlines (in
   Palettes, or the lab's fixture shows them
   in raw MusicXML), not a copy-pasted second
   helping. Playback, parts and any later edit
   all treat the two forms differently, and
   the repeated form edits in one place.

## Hearing it

Press the space bar or the play button.
Playback follows the score, repeats included.
If you hear nothing: check the obvious in
order — system volume, then MuseScore's
**Mixer** (is the staff's channel muted or
turned down?), then chapter 13. What playback
can and cannot prove about your writing is
chapter 7's subject; for now, use it as a
proofreader for wrong notes and broken
rhythms, because it is a very good one: a
wrong pitch *sounds* wrong in a way it does
not look wrong.

## The check

Save your first score, close it, and open it
again. Then export it once — **File → Export**
to PDF — and look at the page as a stranger
would. That round trip (write, save, reopen,
print) is the whole lifecycle in miniature;
every later chapter is one part of it, taken
slowly.
